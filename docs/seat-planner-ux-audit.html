Executive Summary

A genuinely thoughtful product, two fixes away from feeling premium

The Office Seat Planner is well above the average internal tool. Its mental model — a strict separation between an admin draft and a read-only published viewer — is reinforced everywhere, and the publish-review, rules engine, search, undo system, and grounded "Ask Planner" assistant are genuinely strong. What holds it back from world-class is a small number of high-impact issues: a server crash when re-assigning an already-seated employee, a floating inspector that covers the very map it's editing, a two-state-only zoom that won't scale, and a handful of data-model and accessibility gaps. None require architectural change to fix.

Biggest Strengths

The 10 things this product gets right

These are not box-ticks — each is a deliberate design decision that lands, and several exceed what comparable enterprise tools ship.

  1. The publish review is best-in-class. Before anything reaches viewers, a modal itemizes the diff by category (People / Seat inventory / Layout / Metadata), gives Added/Updated/Removed tallies, shows "Draft 60 vs Published 60 · Total changes 1", lists each change as a before→after ("C01 · Open → Jordan Lee"), and warns that publishing clears undo history. This is the confidence ritual Stripe built its reputation on. It directly answers "would you trust yourself to publish?" — yes.
  2. Draft vs Published separation is reinforced relentlessly. Every state — inspector "Draft-only impact" card, every confirmation ("viewers keep seeing the published map until publish"), the PUBLISHED/READ-ONLY badges on the viewer — teaches the same mental model. Users never have to wonder "did this just go live?"
  3. A transparent rules engine explains why. The inspector's ACTIONS / RULES section doesn't just disable Delete — it says "BLOCKED: Assigned seats cannot be deleted. Vacate the seat first," and distinguishes PROTECTED ORIGINAL from CUSTOM DRAFT seats. The system teaches its own constraints.
  4. Command search is fast, multi-entity, and informative. One bar searches people, seats, titles, departments, zones. A single match auto-zooms and ring-highlights; a broad match ("west") returns a live count with a status breakdown (6 assigned · 14 open · 0 reserved) and a scannable result list. Non-matches dim. This is Linear-grade search behavior.
  5. "Ask Planner" is a grounded, honest AI assistant. It answered "which seats are open?" with an accurate per-zone breakdown summing exactly to 54, tagged HIGH CONFIDENCE, and honestly warned it couldn't highlight a broad answer. It is explicitly read-only ("cannot change the map"). Most teams ship AI that hallucinates; this one is scoped and trustworthy.
  6. Reversibility is everywhere. Global undo/redo, per-action toast undo ("Undo Assign C01"), and Discard edits all coexist. I deliberately broke things and always had a clean way back.
  7. Destructive actions are consistently guarded. Delete, Vacate, Swap, and Deactivate each open a confirmation that states the exact impact and reassures that viewers are unaffected until publish. The pattern is identical across surfaces — predictable safety.
  8. Accessibility fundamentals are unusually solid. All 69 buttons are labeled, seats are real buttons whose aria-labels carry status in text ("C01: Open seat. available seat."), the floor image has alt text, lang is set, and keyboard focus is clearly visible. Better than most production SaaS.
  9. Management ships a real audit trail. Publish History lists every completed publish with timestamp, seat count, and the publishing admin's identity — the kind of accountability enterprise buyers ask for on day one.
  10. The map is credible and privacy-aware. A realistic rendered floor plan (not an abstract grid) makes "where do I actually sit" legible, and a one-click "Hide names" lets an admin present the map on a shared screen without exposing people. Thoughtful.

Biggest Weaknesses

The 10 things I flagged — and where they stand

Ordered by impact. Three are resolved or clarified after your review (marked Resolved / Placeholder / Intentional); the remaining seven are the real work, and the top item is the one true blocker.

  1. Re-assigning an already-seated employee crashes the app. Critical Selecting an existing person (who already has a seat) for a new seat and saving produced a raw "error occurred in the Server Components render… a digest property is included" banner — developer text shown to an office admin — and the inspector mislabeled it "Review / fix the highlighted inspector fields" when nothing is fillable. There is no graceful "this person already sits at W11 — move them?" guard. This is the single most damaging issue: the most natural way to move someone breaks.
  2. Admin authentication — confirmed in place. Resolved Initially flagged because my session was pre-authenticated; you've confirmed /admin sits behind a password + magic-link sign-in. No action needed — kept here so the record is accurate.
  3. The inspector covers the map it is editing. High It floats over the right ~25% of the canvas (hiding the East/Northeast pods) while a pinned header, pinned impact card, and pinned footer squeeze the actual working area into a ~250px scroll window — so for an open seat, the employee-name field (the #1 task) sits below the fold and isn't auto-focused. The map is meant to be the "source of spatial truth," yet the panel hides part of it.
  4. Zoom is two fixed states; there is no real canvas navigation. High Only Overview ↔ Detail, no continuous zoom, no +/- controls, no wheel-zoom, and no click-drag panning (drag does nothing). At 60 seats it's survivable; at 1,000–5,000 it breaks down — dense markers can't be read or clicked.
  5. Departments are placeholder data right now. Placeholder The "0 employees" counts and unlinked department text are dummy data, not a defect. The one thing to carry forward: wire the employee→department relation when real departments load, so the filter and counts go live.
  6. Added employees persist through Undo — by design. Intentional You confirmed this is deliberate: a person created during assignment is removed via Management → Deactivate, not Undo. Worth a little in-product signposting so it never reads as a bug, but not a defect.
  7. Panels behave inconsistently. Medium Filters docks on the left and pushes the map; the inspector, Map tools, and Ask Planner overlay from the right. Two paradigms on one screen; open Filters + inspector together and the map is a narrow strip.
  8. Dynamic feedback is invisible to screen readers. Medium The UX leans on toasts and status badges ("Saved changes to C01", SAVING, ERROR, live result counts), but there are zero aria-live regions, so none of it is announced.
  9. The inspector repeats itself and overloads its footer. Medium The "unsaved changes" warning shows twice at once; mid-edit the footer presents five competing actions (Cancel, Assign, Swap, Delete, Discard) and the commit button is called "Assign employee" while the helper text calls it "Save draft changes."
  10. One product wearing three outfits. Low The viewer is branded "Office Seat Finder" but its browser tab title says "Office Seat Planner"; admin (dark rail), viewer (light), and Management (dark header + light cards) are three visual systems; primary buttons alternate red and orange; and some actions fail silently (placing a seat outside a zone, clicking "Spatial confirmation").

Cognitive Load Report

Where users have to stop and think

🔍 Where they hesitate

  • Opening an open seat: the panel appears but the name field is scrolled out of view and unfocused — a beat of "where do I type?"
  • Add-seat mode: no zone outlines, so the first click is a guess; an invalid click gives no feedback.
  • "Spatial confirmation": a control that does nothing on click invites repeated, confused clicking.
  • Footer mid-edit: five actions plus a label/CTA mismatch force a re-read before committing.

😬 Where they lose confidence

  • The server-error banner: the single biggest confidence hit — raw "Server Components render… digest" text where a friendly "couldn't move that person" message belongs.
  • A new person who persists after Undo (intentional) can momentarily read as a bug until the admin learns it's managed in the Directory — a one-line hint would remove the doubt.
  • Two product names ("Planner" vs "Finder") cause a flicker of "is this the same system?" — resolved by the move to one name, "Megeredchian Law Seats."

🔁 Redundant effort

  • Undo/Redo/Ask Planner/Management appear in both the top bar and Map tools.
  • The "unsaved changes" warning renders twice in the inspector at once.
  • Scrolling a cramped 250px window to reach fields that a docked panel could show at once.

🧠 Net assessment

Baseline cognitive load is low-to-moderate — the explainer copy and consistent confirmations do a lot of teaching. The spikes are concentrated and fixable: focus the right field, outline zones, humanize the error, and connect departments. Remove those four and the everyday experience becomes close to effortless.

Redesign Proposal

Same architecture, sharper experience

None of this changes the product's principles — the map stays the source of truth, admin stays workflow-led, viewer stays search-led, draft and published stay separate. These are refinements to layout, hierarchy, and interaction that the Figma frames render in full fidelity.

1 · The Inspector — dock it, auto-collapse it, focus it, simplify it

● Today
  • Floats over the map; right pods disappear behind it.
  • Header + impact card + footer pin a tiny scroll window.
  • The assign field — the main task — starts below the fold.
● Proposed
  • Docked side panel resizes the canvas — the map is never covered.
  • Auto-collapsible — collapses to a slim rail on demand (and when idle), handing full width back to the map.
  • Assignment field first and auto-focused — type immediately.
  • One primary CTA ("Save to draft"); Swap/Delete tuck into "···".

2 · Overall layout & map controls — one panel system, a real canvas

● Today
  • Left panel pushes, right panel overlays — two paradigms.
  • Two open panels squeeze the map to a strip.
  • Two-state zoom; no pan, no fit, no clustering.
● Proposed
  • One docking side panel (tabs: Inspector · Filters · Tools · Ask) — consistent, never overlaps.
  • Map controls: +/- , fit, scroll-zoom, drag-pan, marker clustering.
  • ⌘K promotes the already-strong search to a command palette.

3 · The rest, in brief

Toolbar & search

Collapse the duplicated controls (Undo/Redo/Ask/Management live in two places). Keep one toolbar row; fold secondary tools into the side-panel "Tools" tab. Make search a persistent ⌘K palette that can also trigger Assign/Move/Swap by name.

Publish flow

Already excellent — keep the diff. Add a post-publish rollback (restore any history entry into a new draft) so "publishing clears undo" is no longer a one-way door, and turn each "Previous" row into an action.

Management

Make department a real relation so counts and filters work; unify "Title/Position/Department" to one vocabulary; add bulk select; offer a "merge / clean up" tool for duplicate or orphaned people.

Visual hierarchy

One accent for primary actions (drop the red/orange mix), real headings instead of all-caps labels, and a single shared token set so admin, viewer, and Management read as one product — and one name.

Interaction model

Keep guided Add/Move/Swap modes for precision, but add direct manipulation (drag to pan, optional drag-to-move with snap-to-zone) so the canvas meets users' Figma/Maps instincts.

Design system

Codify the (genuinely good) confirmation, badge, and explainer-copy patterns into reusable components with documented states, and standardize the side-panel, button, and status-color tokens.

Final Verdict

Candid, evidence-based

Would this pass a senior product-design review at a world-class software company?

Not yet — but it's close. The concept, information architecture, copy, and the publish/draft discipline are at or above bar. A senior reviewer would block sign-off on four things: the reproducible crash on the most natural "move someone" path, the unverified admin authentication, the canvas that can't scale past a small floor, and the screen-reader-invisible feedback. None are conceptual failures; all are addressable.

Would you ship it today?

For this office — yes, once the crash is fixed. At ~60 seats and ~8 people it's already a pleasant, capable tool, and the safety rails (drafts, confirmations, audit trail) make it hard to do real damage. As a multi-tenant enterprise product — not yet: the zoom ceiling, bulk-edit absence, auth question, and accessibility gaps would surface immediately at 500+ seats.

What keeps it from feeling best-in-class?

Three things, in order: (1) the inspector fights the map instead of complementing it; (2) the canvas isn't a real canvas (no zoom/pan/drag) the way Figma/Maps/Miro condition users to expect; and (3) the visual layer isn't yet wearing the brand — three slightly different looks, two product names, and a crash that dumps framework text all puncture an otherwise premium impression. Now that the official Megeredchian Law system and the "Megeredchian Law Seats" name are going on, (3) is largely a rollout exercise. Best-in-class software earns trust by never breaking the fourth wall; fix the crash and unify the skin and this one stops doing so.

If you had two weeks, what would have the highest impact?

Spend the fortnight on the top six, in order:

  1. Kill the double-booking crashDetect the existing seat and offer "move / keep both / cancel"; never render framework error text.
  2. Confirm the authentication modelVerify the admin route is protected; add RBAC if not.
  3. Dock & refocus the inspectorStop overlaying the map; put the assignment field first and auto-focused; one primary CTA.
  4. Give the map a real canvasContinuous zoom, fit, and click-drag pan — the biggest single step toward feeling modern and scaling up.
